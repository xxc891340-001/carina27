## Problem Statement

First-time UK renters — especially international students booking from abroad — open 5–10 websites and spend 30–60 minutes researching a single property. They can find crime rates, EPC ratings, flood risk, council tax, planning applications. They cannot interpret any of it. They don't know if a crime rate is high, what EPC D means for their energy bills, or whether a nearby planning application should concern them.

**The problem is not missing data. The problem is missing interpretation.**

Users do not struggle to find data. Users struggle to understand what deserves attention.

Their core question is not: "What is the crime rate?"
Their core question is: "Can I trust this place?"

The highest-priority design problem is therefore not the data pipeline. It is the UX of interpretation: what Lucy sees first, which findings deserve visual emphasis, where upgrade prompts appear naturally, and how findings translate into renter-friendly language. These four design decisions are what this document is for.

## Target User

**Lucy**, 22, Chinese student, first UK rental, booking from abroad.

- Found 2–3 properties on Rightmove and Spareroom
- Spends 30–60 minutes per property across: Rightmove, Google Maps, Crystal Roof, Police Crime Map, Reddit, Xiaohongshu, WeChat groups
- Doesn't understand: Council Tax, EPC, TDS deposit protection, planning applications, UK area differences, noise risk
- Afraid of: renting in a bad area, getting scammed, paying a deposit for the wrong property, discovering problems after moving in, making an expensive mistake because she doesn't know what she doesn't know
- She is not looking for more data. She wants confidence. She wants someone to tell her: "Is this property worth considering, and what should I check before paying?"

## Demand Evidence

- Firsthand observation of this exact struggle from multiple international students
- 5–10 tab cobbled workflow documented in detail; 30–60 min per property; workflow ends with "I'm still not sure"
- £9.8M lost to rental fraud in 13 months (UK, to September 2024); 4,675 reported cases
- 57% of students encountered a rental scam
- 75% of rental fraud victims are young people; 18–29 accounts for 48% of all reported cases

## Status Quo

Typical workflow (5–10 websites, 30–60 min per property):
1. Find property on Rightmove / Zoopla / Spareroom / student accommodation site
2. Google Maps the postcode for area feel
3. Open multiple websites: Crystal Roof (crime, area stats), Police Crime Map, Google Reviews, NHS GP finder, Flood Risk Map, Ofcom mobile coverage, council planning portal
4. Search Reddit, Xiaohongshu, WeChat groups for community experience
5. Ask friends, classmates, existing tenants
6. Some pay a relocation service or letting agent for guidance

Outcome: still uncertain. Ends with a decision made under anxiety, not confidence.

## Competitive Landscape

- **Crystal Roof** — area tool, not a property/verdict tool. **Presentation benchmark.** Their primary strength is not the data: it is visual hierarchy, typography hierarchy, use of colour, highlighting of important information, large map-first layout, and clear separation between primary and secondary content. ProCheck should meet this presentation standard while adding interpretation that Crystal Roof does not provide.
- **PostcodeCheck (~£4.99/report)** — closest direct revenue competitor. Combines EPC, HMO compliance, deposit registration, rent benchmarking, broadband, bills. Weakness: isolated reports with no continuity. Lucy has to buy a new report each time she checks a property. No persistent workspace. No research journey. Reports are sometimes inconsistent or incomplete. This is the specific gap ProCheck addresses.
- **Locrating** — school-centric, aimed at families buying, not tenants renting. Information architecture benchmark.
- **Rightmove / Zoopla** — marketplaces. Not due-diligence tools.
- **PRS landlord database** — government source launching late 2026 (Renters' Rights Act). Will commoditise landlord compliance checks. Not yet available.

**Competitive gap:** No product combines area/property interpretation + persistent research workspace for international renters. The opportunity is not better data aggregation. The opportunity is better interpretation, better prioritisation, better trust-building, and a better research workflow.

## Agreed Premises

1. **Core value = interpretation, not data access.** Lucy can find crime data. She cannot tell if it's bad. ProCheck's job is to translate data into a verdict, not to show more data.

2. **International students booking from abroad = sharpest wedge.** They can't visit, don't have a UK network, face scam risk at deposit stage, and have no fallback. Pain is most acute here.

3. **The conversion event is "Lucy needs certainty right now."** Not specifically at deposit stage. The product supports the whole search journey. Free tier keeps her in the workspace. Paid unlocks deeper interpretation when her confidence threshold isn't met. A subscription or per-report model loses her during the research phase.

4. **PostcodeCheck = direct revenue competitor; Crystal Roof = presentation benchmark; Locrating = information architecture benchmark.** Beat PostcodeCheck on interpretation quality and workspace continuity. Meet Crystal Roof's presentation standard. Reference Locrating's information architecture.

5. **PRS database (late 2026) = tailwind and threat.** When government provides landlord compliance checks, that specific feature gets commoditised. Moat must be in interpretation and trust UX, not data sourcing.

6. **Workspace is the product model, not a feature.** Direct observation of PostcodeCheck revealed: no continuity across multiple properties, no saved research journey, no comparison workflow, data inconsistencies and errors across reports. The core insight is that users evaluate multiple properties over time. ProCheck's differentiation is the persistent workspace that holds Lucy's entire search journey — not a better single report.

## ProCheck Scope Boundary

**ProCheck is an area and property risk assessment tool.** It receives postcode or address input and returns interpretation of publicly available data for that location.

ProCheck can assess (from postcode/address data):
- Crime risk and context
- Noise risk and planning/construction activity
- EPC and energy cost implications
- Flood risk
- Transport accessibility
- Area-level tenant experience signals
- Property-level compliance signals (deposit scheme, EPC grade, gas safety reference)

**ProCheck cannot detect listing-level fraud.** That requires different inputs: listing links, contracts, screenshots, payment requests, landlord details, conversation history. Scam detection at the listing level is a separate product/workflow — it is not in ProCheck scope.

Scam-adjacent signals ProCheck *can* surface from postcode data:
- Price outlier: asking rent significantly below local median (requires rent benchmark data)
- No TDS registration found for the landlord's address
- No gas safety certificate reference in listing text

These are compliance signals, not fraud signals. Communicate them as such.

The bottleneck is not engineering. The bottleneck is UX decisions: information hierarchy, interpretation rules, upgrade triggers, user language. A persistent workspace where Lucy can save properties, return over her full search journey, and access progressively deeper interpretation as she needs more certainty.

Phase 1 (1–2 weeks):

- Saved property cards (postcode + nickname, stored client-side)

## Design Answers: The Four Core UX Questions

### 1. Information Hierarchy — What Lucy Sees First, Second, Third

**First (above the fold, always free):**
The verdict headline. One sentence that directly answers her question before she sees any data.

> "This area is generally safe and well-connected. A few things worth knowing before paying."

or

> "This area has some concerns worth looking at before you commit."

The verdict is a signal, not a score. No number. A judgement in plain English.

**Second (below the fold, still free):**
The 2–3 most decision-relevant signals for her specific situation — not all the data, only what shifts the verdict. Ranked by importance, not by category.

Examples:
- "Crime here is slightly above average for inner London, driven mainly by bike theft. Violent crime is low."
- "This property is a 12-minute walk from [inferred university / city centre]. Good transport links on [lines]."
- "Flood risk: Low. The last recorded flood event in this area was over 15 years ago."

Each signal gets a one-line context sentence. No raw numbers without benchmarks.

**Third (locked behind paywall):**
Property-level analysis that answers the questions Lucy can't answer herself:
- Is this specific property energy-efficient? (EPC interpretation + estimated bills)
- Is there anything happening nearby that will affect living here? (planning applications — severity flagged; see Planning Data complexity note)
- Any compliance concerns with this property? (deposit scheme check, gas safety cert reference, EPC grade)
- What should I ask the landlord before signing? (rule-based Q&A list — see Q&A Logic spec below)

Note: Rent benchmarking vs. local median is a Phase 2 feature. Not in Phase 1 paid tier.

**What to fold / never show unprompted:**
Raw data tables, mobile coverage breakdown by operator, exact NHS GP address lists, historic planning application archives, Ofsted school tables. Show the interpretation. Link to the source if the user wants to verify.

### 2. Interpretation Hierarchy — Which Findings Deserve Visual Emphasis

The Crystal Roof observation is the design brief: visual hierarchy, typography hierarchy, colour, clear highlighting, large map-first layout, primary vs. secondary separation. Apply this to interpretation, not just layout.

**Label vocabulary (canonical — use consistently throughout the product):**
- Verdict (property/area level): **PROCEED** / **LOOK DEEPER** / **SERIOUS CONCERNS**
- Signal severity (individual data points): **MINOR** / **NOTABLE** / **SERIOUS**
- Never mix these vocabularies. Verdict labels appear in headlines. Signal severity labels appear on individual data cards.

**Visual hierarchy rules:**
- SERIOUS CONCERNS verdict: large, prominent, high-contrast treatment (red or amber). Lucy must not miss it.
- PROCEED verdict: positive, confident treatment — not buried or hedged.
- SERIOUS individual signals: card with border, prominent label, action prompt visible without scrolling.
- MINOR individual signals: subdued treatment, no alarm. Below the fold is fine.

**No-data state pattern:**
When a postcode has no data for a given dimension (EPC not found, no flood risk record, no planning history, no police data):
> "We don't have [X] data for this address yet. Here's what to check directly: [link to GOV.UK source]."
Never show a blank card or an error. Always give Lucy an action.

**Interpretation rules:**

Rule 1 — Every data point gets a context sentence.
Not "Crime rate: 45/1,000." Instead: "Crime here runs slightly above the London average, driven mainly by petty theft. Violent crime is below average."

Rule 2 — Use relative benchmarks, never raw numbers.
Lucy has no baseline for UK crime rates, energy costs, or flood return periods. Every metric needs a comparison: "higher than typical for this type of area," "roughly £100–130/month extra in heating vs. EPC B," "lower than 85% of similar postcodes."

Rule 3 — Verdict before data, always.
The PROCEED / LOOK DEEPER / SERIOUS CONCERNS verdict appears at the top of each section before any supporting data. Never make Lucy read four paragraphs to find out if she should be worried.

Rule 4 — Write like a knowledgeable friend.
Avoid all UK housing jargon on first use without explanation:
- Not "EPC D" → "The energy rating (D out of A–G) means this property costs more to heat than average"
- Not "Article 4 direction" → "This area has planning restrictions that may affect how the property can be used or extended"
- Not "TDS protected" → "The deposit for this property appears to be registered in a government scheme, which means it's protected if there's a dispute"

Rule 5 — End every section with one action.
- "Ask the landlord: when was the boiler last serviced?"
- "Check: does your tenancy agreement mention the deposit protection scheme name?"
- "Visit in person before signing — this area has elevated listing fraud this year."

### 3. Upgrade Placement — Natural Triggers, Not Banner Ads

Two upgrade moments in Phase 1 (Trigger C is Phase 2):

**Trigger A: Specific concern found in free tier.**
When the free area verdict surfaces a NOTABLE or SERIOUS signal, the property unlock appears as a direct consequence.

> "We found a planning application submitted nearby that may affect this property. Unlock the full property report to understand what it means and whether to proceed."

The upgrade is a direct answer to a specific question Lucy has right now.

**Trigger B: After the free verdict confirms the area is fine.**
If Lucy reaches the bottom of the free tier feeling positive, she's closest to paying a deposit. The unlock appears as a final due-diligence prompt:

> "The area looks good. Before you pay the deposit, here are 4 things we'd check about this specific property."

She's most confident — and most motivated — to pay right here.

**Trigger C: Comparison threshold (Phase 2 only).**
When Lucy saves a third property and comparison is available:
> "You've saved 3 properties. Compare them side by side — costs, risks, EPC, transport, verdict."

**What NOT to do:**
- No generic "Upgrade to Pro" banner on every page
- No upgrade prompt before Lucy has seen any free value
- No paywall on the area verdict — area-level confidence is what brings her back

### 4. User Language — Communicating Confidence, Risk, and Warnings

**Voice:** A knowledgeable friend who knows UK renting inside out, not a compliance system.

**For confidence (PROCEED):**
"This property looks solid for most renters in your situation. The area is well-connected, the energy rating is reasonable, and we didn't find any significant concerns. Here's what to double-check before signing."

**For moderate concern (LOOK DEEPER):**
"There are a couple of things worth understanding before you commit. Nothing that necessarily means walk away — but you'll want to go in informed. Here's what we found and what to ask."

**For serious concerns (SERIOUS CONCERNS):**
"We found something significant with this property that you should know about before paying anything. [Specific issue in plain English.] We'd recommend asking about this directly — or reconsidering."

**For compliance signals (scam-adjacent):**
"This property shows some signals worth checking before you pay. The rent is lower than similar properties in this area, and we couldn't find a deposit protection registration for this address. These aren't proof of a problem, but they're worth confirming with the landlord before handing over any money."

**Tone principles:**
- Never alarmist — Lucy is already anxious
- Never dismissive — her concerns are valid
- Always specific — never "there may be concerns" without naming what they are
- Always actionable — every warning ends with what to do next
- Bilingual: verdict terms and section headings in EN/ZH Phase 1; body copy stays EN Phase 1

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Minimal Verdict Engine
Single-page app: postcode → free area teaser → paywall → PROCEED/CAUTION/AVOID with plain-English reasoning. No accounts, no workspace. Validates willingness to pay. Ships in 3–5 days. Effort: S. Risk: Low. Weakness: doesn't test the workspace thesis; weak differentiation from PostcodeCheck.

### Approach B: Freemium Research Workspace (Recommended)
Next.js SPA, localStorage persistence (no auth on day 1), property cards saved client-side, free area summaries from cached public APIs, paid property-level unlocks via per-property API calls + Claude API interpretation pass. Free tier = pre-cached area aggregates; paid tier = live per-property lookups + interpretation. Addresses PostcodeCheck's known weakness (no workspace continuity). Ships Phase 1 in 1–2 weeks given existing prototypes and research. Effort: L. Risk: Medium.

### Approach C: API-first + Bot Frontend
Build interpretation engine as API first; Telegram/WhatsApp bot as frontend. No UI to build. Direct distribution into channels Lucy uses. Delays web workspace. Effort: M. Risk: Medium. Weakness: WeChat bot requires China developer account; most UK students expect a web product.

## Q&A Logic Spec (Tailored Landlord Questions)

Rule-based: each signal type maps to a specific question. LLM formats the output into natural language but the mapping is deterministic.

| Signal found | Question generated |
|---|---|
| EPC D or below | "Can you provide the current EPC certificate? What is the estimated monthly heating cost?" |
| No gas safety cert reference | "Can you share the current Gas Safety Certificate? When was the last boiler service?" |
| Planning application nearby | "There's a planning application near this property. Can you tell me what it involves and whether it will affect the property?" |
| Price below local median | "The rent is lower than similar properties nearby. Can you explain why? Are there any issues with the property I should know about?" |
| Short tenancy history / new listing | "How long has this property been available? Who were the previous tenants and why did they leave?" |
| No TDS reference | "Which government deposit protection scheme will you use? Can you give me the scheme name and their reference number after I pay?" |

Default questions (always shown regardless of signals):
- "Who is responsible for repairs, and what is the typical response time?"
- "Is the property licensed, and can I see the HMO licence if applicable?"
- "What is included in the rent — bills, broadband, council tax?"

## Planning Data Complexity Note

UK planning portal data is NOT available via a single national API. Coverage varies by local council. Options:

1. **Planning.data.gov.uk** — DLUHC's centralised planning data platform. Partial coverage (~60% of English councils as of 2025). Free. Recommended starting point.
2. **Third-party aggregator (e.g., Nimbus Maps, LandInsight)** — paid, better coverage, £££. Not viable for Phase 1 cost model.
3. **Council-specific scraping** — brittle, high maintenance. Do not use.

Phase 1 decision: Use Planning.data.gov.uk. For postcodes where data is unavailable, show the no-data state. Do not promise planning data as a reliable feature in Phase 1 marketing.
